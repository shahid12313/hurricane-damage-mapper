1. What a program is

A computer does exactly what it is told, one instruction at a time, and nothing more. A program is a list of those instructions written in a programming language, like a very precise recipe. This project is written in Python, a language popular for science and AI because it reads almost like English.

The instructions live in plain text files ending in .py, inside the src folder. src/train.py, for example, is 170 lines of instructions for teaching the building-finding model. Nothing happens until you run a file, which you do by typing a command in the terminal.

4. What “AI” and “learning” mean here

Normally a programmer writes the rules: if the roof is missing, the building is destroyed. But nobody can write rules precise enough for thousands of satellite photos: shadows, trees, flood water and different roof colours all look different. Machine learning turns this around: instead of rules, we give the computer examples with the right answers, and it adjusts itself until its answers match.

Think of a model as a machine with millions of knobs. Each knob slightly changes how it turns a photo into an answer. At the start the knobs are set randomly, so the answers are nonsense. Learning is the process of turning the knobs, little by little, so the answers get closer to the experts’ answers:

  1. Guess. Show the model a photo; it gives an answer.
  2. Measure the mistake. Compare with the expert’s answer and compute one number for how wrong it was. This number is the loss.
  3. Find which way to turn each knob. For every knob, work out whether turning it up or down would make the loss smaller. This direction is the gradient.
  4. Turn every knob a little in that direction. How far is the learning rate.
  5. Repeat with the next photos, hundreds of thousands of times.

The knobs are called parameters. Your building-finding model has 24,436,369 of them. The next section lets you watch the same process with just one knob.

6. How a computer sees a photo

To a computer, a photo is a grid of numbers. Each square (pixel) holds a brightness from 0 (black) to 255 (white). Here, a bright roof (about 200) sits on dark grass (about 60). A colour photo has three such grids, for red, green and blue.

Each xBD photo is 1,024 × 1,024 pixels, about half a metre of ground per pixel, so a typical house is about 25 × 25 pixels. One photo is 3 million numbers.

A model finds patterns in these numbers with small filters: tiny 3 × 3 grids of knobs that slide over the photo and respond strongly where they find their pattern. One filter might respond to a sharp jump from 60 to 200, which is an edge, like the roof’s border above. The model stacks many layers of filters:

  1. first layers find edges and colours;
  2. middle layers combine edges into corners, lines and textures;
  3. deeper layers combine those into roofs, trees, roads and water;
  4. the last layer decides, for every single pixel, “building” or “not building”.

The model used here is called a U-Net. Its first half shrinks the photo step by step while finding more abstract patterns, and its second half grows it back to full size so it can give an answer for every pixel. It is shaped like a U when drawn, hence the name. Its first half was pretrained on 1.2 million everyday photos, so it already knew edges and textures before seeing a single satellite image.

The answer is itself an image, called a mask: white where the model sees a building, black elsewhere. The damage model gives a mask where each building pixel holds a damage level instead.

7. Your project as a story

The question. After a hurricane, which buildings are damaged, and how badly? People need the answer in hours, not days.

The examples. The xBD dataset has 11,034 pairs of satellite photos, before and after 19 disasters. Experts outlined every building and marked its damage: none, minor, major or destroyed.

Preparing the examples. The photos were stored in a large, slow format (130 GB). We converted them to 9 GB of normal JPEG photos, plus one mask per photo saying what each pixel is.

Teaching the first model to find buildings. It looked at 2,799 before-photos, 8 at a time, comparing its guesses with the experts’ outlines and turning its 24 million knobs a little after each group. One pass through all the photos is an epoch; it did 30 epochs overnight, about 3.5 hours.

Teaching the second model to judge damage. Same idea, but it sees the before and after photo together, so it can compare them, and it answers with a damage level for each building pixel.

Checking honestly. During training we checked on 933 photos the models never learned from (validation) to choose the best version. At the very end, we tested once on 933 more photos that were never used for any choice (test): the final model found buildings with an F1 of 0.79 and gave 80.8% of buildings the right damage level.

Improving. The first damage model rarely recognised minor damage. A second attempt over-corrected and broke major damage; a third, balanced attempt improved the overall score from 0.58 to 0.70 on the test photos.

The map. The models’ answers were turned into building outlines with real-world coordinates and drawn on an interactive map. Doing that revealed that the dataset’s own coordinate tags put buildings about 30 m off, which we measured across all 22,068 photos and corrected.

Concepts

The ideas the code relies on, in the order they build on each other.

Images are arrays of numbers

A colour image of 1024 × 1024 pixels is three grids of numbers, one each for red, green and blue, each from 0 to 255. In code it is an array of shape (1024, 1024, 3). A tensor is the same idea in PyTorch: a multi-dimensional array that can live on the GPU and remember how it was computed. A batch of 8 crops is a tensor of shape (8, 3, 512, 512): 8 images, 3 colour channels, 512 rows, 512 columns.

Supervised learning

The model learns from examples where the answer is known: for every xBD tile, experts drew each building and gave it a damage level. Training shows the model an image, compares its answer with the expert answer, and adjusts the model to be a little less wrong. Repeat hundreds of thousands of times.

Segmentation

Most image models give one answer per image ("cat"). Segmentation gives one answer per pixel ("building", "background"). Both models here are segmentation models; buildings are found afterwards by grouping pixels.

Neural networks and parameters

A neural network is a long chain of simple calculations (multiply by a weight, add, apply a simple non-linear function), with millions of adjustable numbers called parameters or weights. Our models have about 24.4 million. Training means finding parameter values that make the outputs match the answers.

Convolution

Image networks use convolutions: a small grid of weights (for example 3 × 3) slides over the image and computes a weighted sum at each position. Early layers learn to detect edges and colours; deeper layers combine them into corners, roofs and building shapes.

U-Net: encoder and decoder

A U-Net has two halves. The encoder shrinks the image step by step (512 → 256 → … → 16 pixels wide) while learning more abstract features ("roof texture here"). The decoder expands back to full size to give an answer per pixel. Skip connections pass detail from each encoder level to the matching decoder level, so edges stay sharp. Drawn as a diagram, it looks like a U.

ResNet-34 and transfer learning

The encoder is a ResNet-34, a well-known 34-layer network whose "residual" shortcuts make deep networks trainable. Its weights were pretrained on ImageNet, 1.2 million everyday photos, so it already recognises edges, textures and shapes. Starting from that knowledge is called transfer learning; it needs far less data and time than starting from random weights.

Logits, sigmoid and softmax

A network's raw outputs are logits: any numbers, positive or negative. Sigmoid turns one logit into a probability between 0 and 1 (used for building vs background). Softmax turns several logits into probabilities that add up to 1 (used for the five damage classes).

Loss

The loss is one number that says how wrong the model is on a batch; training makes it smaller. Cross-entropy is large when the model gives a low probability to the correct answer. Dice loss measures overlap of areas. Class weights make mistakes on rare classes cost more.

Gradients, backpropagation and the optimizer

For each parameter, the gradient says how the loss changes if that parameter increases a little. Backpropagation computes all 24.4 million gradients efficiently, layer by layer from the output back to the input. The optimizer (here AdamW) then moves every parameter a small step against its gradient. The size of that step is the learning rate (0.0003, later 0.0001).

Batch, iteration, epoch

A batch is a group of examples processed together (8 crops). One iteration is one batch: forward, loss, backward, update. One epoch is one pass over all training tiles: 349 iterations for tier1, 1,146 for tier1 + tier3. Models v1 and the localization model trained for 30 epochs, v2 for 25, v3 for 10.

Augmentation and overfitting

Overfitting is memorising the training examples instead of learning the general pattern; the model then does badly on new images. Augmentation (random crops, flips, rotations) and weight decay reduce it. The "overfit tests" in this project did the opposite on purpose: train on 8 tiles until memorised, just to prove the code can learn at all.

Training, validation and test splits

Data is split three ways. Training data (tier1, later tier3) is what the model learns from. Validation data (hold) is used to choose between options, such as the best epoch or v1 vs v3. Test data is used exactly once at the end; because nothing was chosen with it, its score is the honest estimate of performance on new data.

Precision, recall, F1

Precision: of everything the model called a building, the share that really is. Recall: of all real buildings, the share the model found. F1 = 2 × precision × recall / (precision + recall): high only when both are high. A model that calls everything a building has perfect recall and terrible precision, and a low F1.

The xView2 score and the harmonic mean

Score = 0.3 × localization F1 + 0.7 × damage F1. The damage F1 is the harmonic mean of the four per-level F1 values, which is dominated by the weakest: for example F1 values of 0.9, 0.9, 0.9 and 0.1 give a harmonic mean of only 0.3. This is why ignoring a rare damage level is heavily punished.

Confusion matrix

A table with true classes as rows and predicted classes as columns. Each cell counts how many buildings of that true level got that predicted level. The diagonal holds correct answers; everything else shows which mistakes are made, such as "minor called major".

Checkpoints

A checkpoint is a file with the model's parameters (and, for resuming, the optimizer state and epoch). best.pt is the epoch with the best validation score; last.pt is the latest, for resuming.

GPU, MPS and DataLoader workers

Neural networks are mostly large multiplications, which a GPU does in parallel. On Apple Silicon, PyTorch uses the GPU through MPS. While the GPU computes, DataLoader workers (separate processes) read and prepare the next images on the CPU, so the GPU never waits.

Georeferencing, WKT, GeoJSON, affine transforms

Georeferencing means knowing where on Earth each pixel is. WKT and GeoJSON are text formats for shapes. An affine transform converts pixel (x, y) to (longitude, latitude) with six numbers: it can shift, scale, rotate and shear. A degree of longitude is about 111 km at the equator but only 96 km at Houston (30°N), because longitude lines meet at the poles; ignoring that is exactly the mistake in the GeoTIFF tags.

One training step, traced

What happens in one iteration of the localization model, with the actual shapes:

  1. Load. The DataLoader asks the dataset for 8 random tiles. Each is cropped to 512 × 512, flipped or rotated at random, and normalised. Stacked together: images (8, 3, 512, 512), masks (8, 1, 512, 512) of 0s and 1s.
  2. Move. Both tensors are copied to the GPU (.to("mps")).
  3. Forward pass. The encoder shrinks each crop through 5 levels down to 16 × 16 × 512 features; the decoder expands back to 512 × 512 using the skip connections. Output: logits (8, 1, 512, 512), one score per pixel.
  4. Loss. Binary cross-entropy compares all 2,097,152 pixel scores (8 × 512 × 512) with the mask; Dice compares predicted and true areas per image. Their sum is one number, for example 0.7.
  5. Backward pass. loss.backward() computes a gradient for each of the 24,436,369 parameters.
  6. Update. AdamW moves each parameter a tiny step (scaled by the learning rate, 0.0003) to lower the loss.
  7. Repeat. 349 times is one epoch (about 5 minutes); then validation on 933 full tiles (about 1.5 minutes); then save checkpoints; then the next epoch, with a slightly lower learning rate.

The damage model is the same except the input is (8, 6, 512, 512) (before and after stacked), the output is (8, 5, 512, 512) (five class scores per pixel), the target is (8, 512, 512) whole numbers 0–4 (or 255 to ignore), and the loss is weighted cross-entropy.

def parse_wkt_polygon(wkt):

def polygon_area(points):

def dice_loss(logits, target, eps=1.0):
    prob = torch.sigmoid(logits)
    inter = (prob * target).sum(dim=(1, 2, 3))
    union = prob.sum(dim=(1, 2, 3)) + target.sum(dim=(1, 2, 3))
    return (1 - (2 * inter + eps) / (union + eps)).mean()

Dice loss measures overlap between predicted and true building areas.

  • sigmoid turns raw scores (logits, any number) into probabilities 0–1.
  • inter: sum of probability × truth = the "overlap"; union: total predicted + total true.
  • sum(dim=(1, 2, 3)) adds over channel, height and width, giving one number per image in the batch.
  • Dice = 2 × overlap / (predicted + true): 1 for perfect overlap. The loss is 1 − Dice, so smaller is better. eps avoids dividing by zero on crops without buildings.

def evaluate(model, loader, device, threshold=0.5):
    model.eval()
    tp = fp = fn = 0
    for images, masks in loader:
        pred = torch.sigmoid(model(images.to(device))) > threshold
        masks = masks.to(device) > 0.5
        tp += (pred & masks).sum().item()
        fp += (pred & ~masks).sum().item()
        fn += (~pred & masks).sum().item()
    precision = tp / max(tp + fp, 1)
    recall = tp / max(tp + fn, 1)
    f1 = 2 * precision * recall / max(precision + recall, 1e-9)
    return {"f1": f1, "precision": precision, "recall": recall}

  • @torch.no_grad() tells PyTorch not to record gradients: faster and uses less memory, since we are not learning here.

  • For each batch: probability above 0.5 means "building". @torch.no_grad()
def building_confusion(model, dataset, device):
    """Building-level 4x4 confusion matrix (rows true, columns predicted).

    Each true building's predicted level is the most common predicted level inside it.
    Buildings are connected regions of the true mask, so touching buildings count as one.
    """
    model.eval()

        x, target = dataset[i]
        _, damage = predict_classes(model(x[None].to(device)))
        damage, target = damage[0].cpu().numpy(), target.numpy()
        n, components = cv2.connectedComponents(((target >= 1) & (target <= 4)).astype(np.uint8))

            pred_level = np.bincount(damage[inside], minlength=5)[1:].argmax()
            matrix[true_level, pred_level] += 1
    return matrix

def extract_buildings(building_prob, level_prob, threshold=0.5):
    """Split the building mask into separate buildings, each with a damage level and outline in pixels."""
    n, components = cv2.connectedComponents((building_prob > threshold).astype(np.uint8))
    buildings = []
    for k in range(1, n):
        inside = components == k
        if inside.sum() < MIN_BUILDING_PIXELS:
            continue
        mean_prob = level_prob[:, inside].mean(axis=1)
        contours, _ = cv2.findContours(inside.astype(np.uint8), cv2.RETR_EXTERNAL, cv2.CHAIN_APPROX_SIMPLE)
        outline = cv2.approxPolyDP(max(contours, key=cv2.contourArea), 1.0, True)[:, 0, :]
        if len(outline) < 3:
            continue
        buildings.append({
            "damage": int(mean_prob.argmax()) + 1,
            "confidence": float(mean_prob.max()),
            "outline": outline.astype(np.float64),
        })
    return buildings

def georeference(label_path, tif_path):
    """Pixel -> (longitude, latitude) function for a tile.

    The label file lists every building both in pixels and in longitude/latitude, which fixes an
    exact affine transform. The GeoTIFF tags are used only for tiles without buildings: they claim
    square pixels in degrees, which is wrong away from the equator and shifts buildings by about
    30 m (up to 84 m; see src/check_georef.py).
    """
    with open(label_path) as f:
        features = json.load(f)["features"]
    pixel_outlines = {feat["properties"]["uid"]: parse_wkt_polygon(feat["wkt"]) for feat in features["xy"]}
    pixels, lnglats = [], []
    for feat in features["lng_lat"]:
        xy, lnglat = pixel_outlines.get(feat["properties"]["uid"]), parse_wkt_polygon(feat["wkt"])
        if xy is not None and len(xy) == len(lnglat):
            pixels.append(xy)
            lnglats.append(lnglat)

    if sum(len(p) for p in pixels) >= 3:
        pixels = np.vstack(pixels)
        coef = np.linalg.lstsq(np.column_stack([pixels, np.ones(len(pixels))]), np.vstack(lnglats), rcond=None)[0]
    else:
        with tifffile.TiffFile(tif_path) as tif:
            tags = tif.pages[0].tags
            scale_x, scale_y = tags["ModelPixelScaleTag"].value[:2]
            lon0, lat0 = tags["ModelTiepointTag"].value[3:5]
        coef = np.array([[scale_x, 0.0], [0.0, -scale_y], [lon0, lat0]])

    def to_lnglat(xy):
        return np.column_stack([xy, np.ones(len(xy))]) @ coef

    return to_lnglat

Converts pixel positions to longitude/latitude, the project's main finding in code. Each label file lists every building twice: in pixels and in longitude/latitude. Lines 85–91 pair them up by building id.

Line 95 is an affine transform fitted by least squares: find the 6 numbers in coef so that [x, y, 1] @ coef ≈ [longitude, latitude] for all corners at once. An affine transform can scale, shift, rotate and shear, which covers how a map relates to a flat photo of a small area. The fit is exact to within 10 cm on every image.

Lines 97–101: tiles with no buildings fall back to the GeoTIFF tags, which are about 30 m off (see check_georef.py). Lines 103–106 return a small function that applies the transform to any points.

def fetch_osm(tile, bbox):
    """OSM building ways inside bbox (south, west, north, east), cached per tile."""
    OSM_CACHE.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
    cache = OSM_CACHE / f"{tile}.json"
    if cache.exists():
        return json.loads(cache.read_text())
    south, west, north, east = bbox
    query = f'[out:json][timeout:60];way["building"]({south},{west},{north},{east});out geom;'
    problem = None
    for attempt in range(6):
        try:
            response = requests.post(OVERPASS, data={"data": query}, headers={"User-Agent": USER_AGENT}, timeout=240)
            if response.status_code == 200:
                elements = response.json()["elements"]
                cache.write_text(json.dumps(elements))
                time.sleep(2)  # be polite to the public server
                return elements
            problem = f"HTTP {response.status_code}"
        except requests.RequestException as error:  # the public server is often slow or busy
            problem = type(error).__name__
        time.sleep(20 * (attempt + 1))
    print(f"  skipped {tile}: Overpass {problem}", flush=True)
    return None

Downloads OSM buildings for a box through the Overpass API, a public query service. Results are cached in data/osm/ so each tile is downloaded once. The server is often busy, so it retries up to 6 times with longer waits, and waits 2 seconds between requests to be polite. A tile that still fails is skipped, not crashed.

def score(points, osm_polys, tree):
    pts = shapely.points(points)
    inside = np.zeros(len(points), bool)

def best_shift(points, tree):
    """Translation (in metres) that puts the most label building centres inside OSM buildings."""
    dx, dy = np.meshgrid(SHIFTS_M, SHIFTS_M)
    shifts = np.column_stack([dx.ravel(), dy.ravel()])
    moved = (points[None, :, :] + shifts[:, None, :]).reshape(-1, 2)
    hits = np.zeros(len(moved), bool)
    hits[np.unique(tree.query(shapely.points(moved), predicate="within")[0])] = True
    counts = hits.reshape(len(shifts), len(points)).sum(axis=1)
    best = counts.max()
    # several shifts can tie; take the one closest to zero
    tied = shifts[counts == best]
    sx, sy = tied[np.argmin(np.hypot(tied[:, 0], tied[:, 1]))]
    return float(sx), float(sy), int(best)

def validate_tile(label_path):
    tile = label_path.stem.replace("_post_disaster", "")
    label_polys, tif_polys = xbd_buildings(label_path)
    all_pts = np.vstack(label_polys + tif_polys)
    bbox = (all_pts[:, 1].min() - MARGIN_DEG, all_pts[:, 0].min() - MARGIN_DEG,
            all_pts[:, 1].max() + MARGIN_DEG, all_pts[:, 0].max() + MARGIN_DEG)
    elements = fetch_osm(tile, bbox)
    origin = (all_pts[:, 0].mean(), all_pts[:, 1].mean())
    if elements is None:
        return {"tile": tile, "disaster": tile.rsplit("_", 1)[0], "lat": origin[1], "xbd_buildings": len(label_polys)}

    osm_polys = []
    for way in elements:
        ring = np.array([[node["lon"], node["lat"]] for node in way.get("geometry", [])])
        if len(ring) >= 4:
            poly = shapely.make_valid(shapely.Polygon(to_metres(ring, origin)))
            if poly.area > 1:
                osm_polys.append(poly)
    row = {"tile": tile, "disaster": tile.rsplit("_", 1)[0], "lat": origin[1],
           "xbd_buildings": len(label_polys), "osm_buildings": len(osm_polys)}
    if len(osm_polys) < 5:
        return row

    tree = shapely.STRtree(osm_polys)
    label_pts, tif_pts = centres(label_polys, origin), centres(tif_polys, origin)
    row["label_hit_rate"], row["label_median_dist_m"] = score(label_pts, osm_polys, tree)

    row["best_shift_x_m"], row["best_shift_y_m"], _ = best_shift(label_pts, tree)
    row["best_shift_m"] = float(np.hypot(row["best_shift_x_m"], row["best_shift_y_m"]))
    row["tif_vs_label_m"] = float(np.median(np.hypot(*(tif_pts - label_pts).T)))

Test yourself

Questions a committee is likely to ask. Answer out loud first, then open the answer.

Why are there two models instead of one?

Finding buildings and judging damage are different tasks. Buildings are clearest in the before image, before any damage; damage needs both images. Splitting them keeps each model's job simple. (In practice the damage model also found buildings slightly better, F1 0.82 vs 0.79, because it sees both images.)

Why train on 512 × 512 crops if tiles are 1024 × 1024?

Four times less memory per example, so a batch of 8 fits on the laptop GPU, and each epoch sees a different random quarter of each tile, which acts as extra variety.

Why normalise with the ImageNet mean and standard deviation?

The encoder's pretrained weights were learned on images normalised that way. Feeding differently scaled numbers would make its learned features less useful at the start.

Why add Dice loss to cross-entropy for the localization model?

About 85% of pixels are background. Cross-entropy counts pixels, so small buildings contribute little. Dice is a ratio of overlapping areas, so a missed small building matters as much as a large stretch of background.

Why did v2 fail?

Minor and major damage are the two levels the model confuses most. With minor weighted 6 and major 3, calling an uncertain building "minor" became the cheaper mistake, so the model almost stopped predicting major. The harmonic mean then pulled the score down.

Why did v3 work?

Equal weights for minor and major meant neither was the safer guess; starting from v1 kept what v1 already knew about major damage; the lower learning rate refined rather than overwrote it; and tier3 tripled the data. v3 changed all of these at once, so the gain cannot be attributed to one change.

Why is v3's building accuracy not higher than v1's, if its score is much higher?

v3 finds far more minor-damage buildings, but also calls about 11–14% of undamaged buildings minor. Undamaged buildings are the large majority, so plain accuracy stays about the same, while the score, which weights all four levels equally, rises sharply.

Why is the test score the one to report?

The hold split was used to choose epochs and versions, so its scores are slightly optimistic. The test split was never used for any decision, so it is the honest estimate of performance on new data.

How do we know the label coordinates are right and the GeoTIFF tags wrong?

Three independent pieces of evidence: the tags use exactly the same degrees for longitude and latitude, which is only correct at the equator; the error grows with latitude exactly as that mistake predicts (1 m at Palu, 49 m in Portugal); and on an independent Esri basemap the label placement lines up while the tag placement shows every building doubled. The OpenStreetMap comparison adds a fourth.

What would you improve next?

A Siamese network (one shared encoder for before and after), splitting touching buildings (watershed), ensembles and test-time augmentation, and fine-tuning on data from the affected region.
